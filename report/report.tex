\section{Problem and findings}
After an election without a majority, parties bargain over who governs and how ministries are split.
Real coalition cabinets divide portfolios almost exactly in proportion to seats (\emph{Gamson's law},
slope $\approx1$ in $p=\alpha+\beta s+\gamma F$), whereas the leading bargaining theory, Baron--Ferejohn
(BF), predicts that the \emph{formateur} (the party proposing the cabinet) captures a large premium:
the \emph{portfolio allocation paradox}. We model parties as reinforcement-learning agents in a
multi-agent environment that encodes government formation, rewarded only by their own office and
policy payoffs (no fairness term), check them against the exact BF equilibrium, then switch
institutional features on and off to find out which ones make the premium disappear. Findings:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{F\arabic*}]
\item \textbf{Validated learner.} In the BF game, learned formateur shares match the closed form within
\EzeroAbsGap{} on average (\EzeroSeeds{} seeds).
\item \textbf{Private information removes the formateur premium.} Making parties' valuations private removes
the formateur premium (from \BasePhi{} to \MfourPhi) and removing it from the full game restores the
premium (\LooMfourPhi): it is sufficient \emph{and} necessary. Policy motivation is also sufficient.
\item \textbf{The premium moves into portfolio quality and shares follow power.} With all mechanisms
the formateur gets no extra ministries ($\tilde\Phi=\EstarPhi$) but takes the ones it values most
(premium \EstarPhiNu{} in its own valuation), and shares track Banzhaf voting power better than seats.
\item \textbf{Transfer to real parliaments.} Without training on real data, agents placed in 242
real post-war Western European parliaments pick the governing coalition as often as classic
size-based theories (\PgLearned{} vs \PgRiker), and their allocations show no formateur premium.
\end{enumerate}

\section{RL formulation}
\textbf{Environment} (\code{cabinet/env.py}): a partially observable stochastic game with $n$ parties
(seats $w_i$, majority quota $q$) and $m$ ministries. Each period without a cabinet is a formation round:
nature draws a formateur $f\sim\rho$, $f$ proposes a winning coalition $C\ni f$ and an allocation
$\mu:\{1..m\}\to C$, and the cabinet forms only if every partner accepts. A cabinet pays per-period flows
until the term ends or a member walks out. Switchable mechanisms (\code{cabinet/config.py}): repeated
terms with elections (M1), private types (M4), heterogeneous ministry values (M5), exits (M8),
ministerial policy (M9), seat-proportional recognition (M10); level E0 is exactly the symmetric BF game.

\textbf{State $\mathcal S$.} Agent $i$ observes a \emph{set} of $n$ party tokens (26 features: seat share,
recognition probability, roles, pending/current shares, last $\Lambda$ cabinets), $m$ ministry tokens
(8: salience, own valuation, policy dimension led), $m{\times}n$ pair features (4; partners' valuations
and policy costs when types are public), a 17-dim context (phase, quota, $\delta$, horizon, $\lambda$,
\ldots) and 8 decision features. Tokens carry no labels: one network serves any $n$ and $m$ and is
permutation-equivariant.

\textbf{Action $\mathcal A$} (masked, discrete). Formateur: include/exclude each other party (masks keep
the coalition winning), then a pointer to a coalition member for each ministry; up to $10^{19}$ proposals
are decoded in $n{-}1{+}m$ steps (a divisible surplus uses a 1\% offer grid per partner). Voter:
accept/reject. Governing member: stay/exit.

\textbf{Reward $\mathcal R$.} In a governed period member $i$ receives
$r_i=\sum_{k:\mu(k)=i}\nu_{ik}-\lambda\frac1d\sum_h|y_h-z_{ih}|$: the value of its ministries minus a
policy loss (the minister holding the lead ministry of dimension $h$ sets $y_h$). Outsiders get the policy
term, an exiting member a bonus, failed rounds nothing; discount $\delta$ per period. Rewards are
individual: a team reward would erase the conflict under study.

\textbf{Algorithm} (\code{cabinet/policy.py}, \code{cabinet/ppo.py}). One shared attention network with
masked autoregressive proposal heads, vote/exit heads and a value head. Agents act only at some steps, so
PPO with GAE runs on each agent's own decision sequence (semi-MDP). Learners: shared-parameter IPPO
self-play (main), MAPPO with a centralised critic, fictitious self-play, PSRO; optional GRU memory.
Baselines (\code{cabinet/baselines.py}): exact BF strategy from our solver (\code{cabinet/bf\_solver.py}),
a Gamson agent, a myopic BF heuristic and a random agent. Metrics (\code{cabinet/metrics.py}):
formateur premium $\tilde\Phi=\mathbb E[p_f-s_f]$ and Gamson distance $\tilde D_G$, floor-adjusted for
indivisible ministries, and regression (1). Scaled to one laptop: $n\le5$ ($3$--$8$ for real
parliaments), $m=6$, 6-period terms, 500--700 PPO iterations (4--6M decisions) per run, $\ge3$ seeds.

\section{Results}
\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../figures/fig_e0_benchmark.pdf}

\textbf{F1, validation.} Learned shares follow the BF closed form (Fig.~\ref{fig:e0}; mean gap
\EzeroGap, \EzeroCellsOK/9 cells within 0.02), including its decrease with patience $\delta$; errors
grow with $n$, where each voter's threshold is learned less precisely. The same code then produces all
other results, so differences between configurations come from the institutions, not the learner.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../figures/fig_mechanisms.pdf}
\caption{F2: formateur premium when one mechanism is added to the base game (blue: sufficiency) or
removed from the full game E* (orange: necessity); mean and 95\% CI over seeds.}\label{fig:mech}
\end{figure}

\textbf{F2, what removes the premium.} The base game keeps the BF premium ($\tilde\Phi=\BasePhi$,
$\hat\gamma=\BaseGamma$). Added alone (Fig.~\ref{fig:mech}, blue), private types ($\MfourPhi$) and
ministerial policy ($\MninePhi$) each remove it and seat-proportional recognition nearly does
($\MtenPhi$), while repetition, exits and heterogeneous values do not. Removed from E* (orange),
private types bring it back ($\LooMfourPhi$); removing values, exits or recognition restores part of it.
The mechanism: when a formateur cannot see what partners value, it must over-offer to secure
acceptance, which erodes its advantage. Repetition fails because agents discount the next term
heavily (with patient agents: \MpatientPhi; with GRU memory as well: \MpatientrecPhi).

\textbf{F3, where the premium goes.} In E* the formateur no longer gets more ministries
($\tilde\Phi=\EstarPhi$, $\hat\gamma=\EstarGamma$) but its premium measured in its own valuation is
$\Phi_\nu=\EstarPhiNu$: it keeps the portfolios it cares about, a ``quality'' advantage that counts of
ministries cannot see. Shares are flatter than seats ($\hat\beta=\EstarBeta$): small partners are
over-paid, the small-party bias of real cabinets, and voting power explains shares better than seats
($R^2$ \EstarRtwoBz{} for Banzhaf shares vs \EstarRtwo{} for seat shares). This is what bargaining
predicts (a pivotal small party is worth as much as a large one), so seat-proportionality itself does not
emerge ($\tilde D_G=\EstarDg$). Table~\ref{tab:algo}: these patterns hold across learners, memory and a
finer 12-ministry game; symmetric PSRO gives \PsroEstarPhi.

\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../figures/fig_parlgov.pdf}

\textbf{F4, real parliaments.} Policies trained only on random parliaments were placed in the 242
post-war Western European majority coalitions of ParlGov, the real prime minister's party proposing
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pg}). They pick the governing coalition in \PgLearned{} of cases, like Riker's minimal-seat
rule (\PgRiker) and a random minimal winning coalition (\PgRandom), and \PgAllLearned{} across \PgAllN{}
coalitions in 33 democracies. The ideologically closest coalition does better (\PgIdeology): in this model a
formateur can keep the policy-setting ministries itself, so partners' ideology barely matters to it,
even when positions are visible (\PgIdeoLearned). On the real seat distributions the learned allocations
show no formateur premium ($\tilde\Phi=\PgPhi$) and follow voting power ($R^2$ \PgRtwoBz{} vs \PgRtwo).

\textbf{Reliability.} Cross-play between independently trained runs costs \CmSame{} (same algorithm) and
\CmAcross{} (across algorithms) per period: runs reach compatible conventions. Best responses gain
\GainEstar{} per period against E* (\GainMappo{} for MAPPO), near the 0.01 tolerance, but they also
barely exploit a scripted Gamson profile (\GainBASEGamson), so we claim approximate, not certified,
equilibrium play.

\section{Implications and limitations}
Learning agents resolve one half of the portfolio paradox: the formateur premium of bargaining theory
disappears once parties have private information (or care about policy), and what remains is a premium
in portfolio \emph{quality}, consistent with the empirical observation that weighting ministries by
importance changes the picture. The other half, exact seat-proportionality, does not emerge from
self-interested bargaining: learned shares follow voting power, which suggests that real cabinets'
seat-proportionality reflects norms or pre-electoral bargaining that our agents lack. Limitations:
scaled-down sizes; exploitability tests can refute but not prove equilibrium; the GRU is trained with
stored states, without back-propagation through time; opponent shaping and the demand and
pre-electoral modules of the specification were not run; ParlGov has no portfolio counts, so real
allocations are compared through coalitions only. Coverage of the specification:
\code{docs/SPEC\_COVERAGE.md}.
