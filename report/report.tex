\section{Problem and findings}
After an election without a majority, parties bargain over who governs and how ministries are split.
Real coalition cabinets divide portfolios almost exactly in proportion to seats (\emph{Gamson's law}):
on \RealN{} real cabinets, share $=\RealA+\RealB\,s+\RealG\,F$ ($s$: seat share in the coalition,
$F$: prime minister's party; $R^2$ \RealRtwo). The leading bargaining theory, Baron--Ferejohn (BF),
predicts instead that the \emph{formateur} (the party proposing the cabinet) captures a large premium.
We model parties as reinforcement-learning agents in a multi-agent environment of government formation,
rewarded only by their own office and policy payoffs (no fairness term), validate them against the
exact BF equilibrium, and test which institutions remove the premium. Findings:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{F\arabic*}]
\item \textbf{Validated learner.} In the BF game learned formateur shares match the closed form within
\EzeroAbsGap{} (\EzeroSeeds{} seeds).
\item \textbf{Under proposal bargaining the premium never disappears; it changes currency.} The base game
gives a formateur coefficient $\hat\gamma=\BaseGamma$. Private valuations or policy motives drive the
\emph{counted} premium to zero, but the formateur keeps it in the ministries it values most or in the
policy-setting ministries.
\item \textbf{Real cabinets have no premium in any currency.} Real prime-minister parties also hold the key
ministries in proportion, and Gamson's law predicts real splits better than any learned agent or BF.
\item \textbf{The protocol is what matters.} Under demand bargaining the agents' own cabinets follow
Gamson's law with the small-party bias of real cabinets (slope \DemB, $\hat\gamma=\DemG$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{RL formulation}
\textbf{Environment} (\code{cabinet/env.py}, \code{cabinet/config.py}): a partially observable stochastic
game with $n$ parties (seats $w_i$, majority quota) and $m$ ministries. In a period without a cabinet a
formateur $f\sim\rho$ proposes a winning coalition $C\ni f$ and an allocation $\mu:\{1..m\}\to C$; the
cabinet forms if every partner accepts and pays per-period flows until the term ends or a member exits.
Switchable mechanisms: repeated terms (M1), demand protocol (M2), private types (M4), heterogeneous
values (M5), exits (M8), ministerial policy (M9), recognition rule (M10); level E0 is the exact BF game.

\textbf{State $\mathcal S$.} A \emph{set} of $n$ party tokens (26 features: seats, recognition
probability, roles, offers, last $\Lambda$ cabinets), $m$ ministry tokens (8: salience, own valuation,
policy dimension led), pair features, a 17-dim context and 8 decision features; no labels, so one
permutation-equivariant network serves any $n,m$.
\textbf{Action $\mathcal A$} (masked): the formateur includes/excludes each party (masks keep the coalition
winning) and points each ministry to a member ($10^{19}$ proposals in $n{-}1{+}m$ steps); voters
accept/reject; members stay/exit; under M2 a party demands a share (1\% grid) or closes.
\textbf{Reward $\mathcal R$.} $r_i=\sum_{k:\mu(k)=i}\nu_{ik}-\lambda\frac1d\sum_h|y_h-z_{ih}|$ per governed
period (value of own ministries minus policy loss; the holder of a lead ministry sets $y_h$), an exit
bonus on leaving, 0 otherwise; discount $\delta$. Rewards are individual.

\textbf{Algorithm} (\code{cabinet/policy.py}, \code{cabinet/ppo.py}): one shared attention network with
masked autoregressive heads, trained by PPO with GAE on each agent's own decision sequence (semi-MDP);
also MAPPO (central critic), fictitious self-play, PSRO (\code{cabinet/psro.py}) and a GRU memory.
Baselines: the exact BF strategy from our solver (\code{cabinet/bf\_solver.py}), Gamson, myopic and random
agents (\code{cabinet/baselines.py}). Metrics (\code{cabinet/metrics.py}): regression (1)
$p=\alpha+\beta s+\gamma F$, its analogues for value and policy-ministry shares, and the floor-adjusted
Gamson distance $\tilde D_G$. Scale: $n\le5$ ($3$--$8$ for real parliaments), $m=6$, 500--2000 PPO
iterations per run, $\ge3$ seeds (10 for the main cells).

\section{Results}
\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../figures/fig_e0_benchmark.pdf}
\caption{F1: learned formateur share (dots) vs the BF closed form (lines) in the symmetric game;
dashed: Gamson's equal split.}\label{fig:e0}
\end{figure}

\textbf{F1, validation.} Learned shares follow the BF closed form (Fig.~\ref{fig:e0}; mean gap
\EzeroGap, \EzeroCellsOK/9 cells within 0.02), including its decrease with patience $\delta$; errors
grow with $n$, where each voter's threshold is learned less precisely. The same code then produces all
other results, so differences between configurations come from the institutions, not the learner.



\textbf{F2, the premium changes currency.} The base game keeps the BF premium ($\hat\gamma=\BaseGamma$),
and it grows with training (\LongBaseG{} after 4$\times$ more updates). Repetition, even with patient
agents and GRU memory, exits and the recognition rule leave it in place (Fig.~\ref{fig:mech}), as do
MAPPO, fictitious self-play and PSRO; a companion implementation of the same question by our team finds
0.20--0.24. In the full game E* every learner and memory variant (IPPO, MAPPO, fictitious self-play,
GRU, memoryless, 12 ministries) gives a counted $\hat\gamma\le\MappoGamma$--$\EstarGamma$ but a value
premium of \LamzeroPhiNu--\FspPhiNu. Two mechanisms remove the \emph{counted} premium: private types
($\hat\gamma=\MfourGamma$) and ministerial policy ($\MnineGamma$), and removing private types from the
full game restores it. But the premium moves: with private valuations the formateur's share of what
\emph{it} values exceeds its seat share by \MfourPhiNu{} (base \BasePhiNu), and with policy it holds the
policy-setting ministries (coefficient \MnineGlead{} on its share of them). The formateur trades the
number of ministries for their quality or for policy control, and this persists with 4$\times$ training
(private types: counted \LongMfourG, value premium \LongMfourPhiNu).

\textbf{F3, real cabinets.} We imposed each real coalition and prime minister (Seki--Williams data linked
to ParlGov) on policies trained only on random parliaments and compared their split with the real one
(Fig.~\ref{fig:real}, \RealNcommon{} cabinets). Gamson's law errs by \MaeGamson, BF by \MaeBF{} (implied
$\hat\gamma=\GcBF$). Agents with private types err by \MaePrivLo--\MaePrivHi{} (implied $\hat\gamma$
\GcPrivLo--\GcPrivHi), much closer to Gamson than the same agents with public types
(\MaePubLo--\MaePubHi, $\hat\gamma$ \GcPubLo--\GcPubHi, like BF), but every one is worse than Gamson's law
(paired CI above \DmaePrivLo). Is the real premium hidden in quality, as in our agents? No: on \KeyN{}
cabinets the prime minister's party holds \KeyShare{} of the key ministries (finance, foreign affairs,
interior, defence, justice) against \KeyCount{} of all ministers, a difference of \KeyDiff{} (95\% CI
\KeyDiffLo{} to \KeyDiffHi). Real seat shares also fit better than voting power ($R^2$ \RealRtwo{} vs
\RealRtwoBz), whereas our agents' shares follow voting power. When the agents choose the coalition
themselves in 242 real parliaments, they pick the governing one in \PgLearned{} of cases, like Riker's
minimal-seat rule (\PgRiker), behind an ideological rule (\PgIdeology).

\textbf{F4, the protocol.} Under demand bargaining (Morelli; each party in turn demands a share or
closes a cabinet with earlier demanders and takes the rest) on the same random parliaments as a matched
proposal game, the formateur coefficient falls from \PropG{} to \DemG{} and the seat slope rises from
\PropB{} to \DemB. Placed in the real parliaments, demand-bargaining agents form cabinets whose regression
matches the real one, small-party bias included (Table~\ref{tab:own}); proposal agents keep the premium.
In symmetric parliaments the learned demands first underpaid the closer (\LockinCloserThree{} of the
pie with 3 parties, \LockinCloserFive{} with 5): an exploration lock-in, since with more exploration of
demands the closer gets \ExploreCloserThree{} and \ExploreCloserFive{}, moving to the equal split
(1/2, 1/3) that theory predicts.

\textbf{Reliability.} A best response trained for 400 PPO iterations gains \GainLong{} per period against
E*, but \GainLongGamson{} and \GainLongMyopic{} against scripted Gamson and BF-heuristic profiles: the
learned profiles are approximate equilibria at our budget, and the test has the power to tell. Cross-play
between independent runs costs \CmSame{} per period.

\section{Implications and limitations}
Self-interested learning under the closed-rule proposal protocol reproduces bargaining theory, not
Gamson's law: whatever the information, memory, exits or learner, the formateur keeps a premium, at
most moving it from the number of ministries to their quality or to policy. Real cabinets show no
premium in counts or in key ministries, so their proportionality is not a premium in disguise. What
changes the outcome is the protocol: when parties make demands instead of answering a formateur's
proposal, learned allocations become seat-proportional. Limitations: scaled-down sizes; exploitability
tests refute but do not prove equilibrium; real allocations count ministers (salience only through the
key-ministry test); the prime minister's party stands in for the formateur. Coverage of the
specification: \code{docs/SPEC\_COVERAGE.md}.
